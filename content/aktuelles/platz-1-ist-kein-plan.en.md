Switzerland tops the Global Innovation Index for the fifteenth consecutive year. 66.0 out of 100, ahead of Sweden, the United States, Korea, and Singapore. It is a remarkable result. It is also a description of the past.

Rankings measure outputs. Outputs come from decisions made ten to fifteen years earlier. What we see today on the index is the harvest of the Swiss education, research, and industrial fabric of the early 2010s. What we do not see is the substrate that will produce the next twenty years. That substrate appears in no ranking, because rankings are lagging indicators. By the time the score drops, the damage is already a decade old.

---

## What the rankings do not capture

Innovation has a physical and institutional underlay. Six building blocks will be decisive over the next ten years:

1. **Sovereign compute capacity.** Anyone serious about developing AI needs datacentres on home soil, with own energy and own hardware procurement. Switzerland has neither a strategic inventory nor a political target here.
2. **Supply-chain proximity.** Semiconductors, precision machines, specialty chemistry, critical raw materials. Distance from the source is a risk that shows up in no innovation indicator.
3. **Energy and manufacturing headroom.** Innovation does not scale without surplus energy capacity and manufacturing reserves. Both have become scarce across Europe.
4. **Talent inflow.** Top international researchers, engineers, and founders decide in life-years, not in legislative terms. Obstacles we erect today land in 2035.
5. **Anti-lock-in procurement.** When public administration, hospitals, and schools tie themselves to single vendors, innovation becomes a licence fee. Open standards and interchangeable components are a leading indicator of innovative capacity, not a footnote.
6. **Dense expertise clusters.** Research strength comes from neighbourhood, not subsidy. Clusters take decades to grow and dissolve faster than they form.

None of these six is visible in the GII score of 66.0.

---

## Where Switzerland is weak

The GII 2025 decomposes the overall score into seven pillars. Switzerland ranks first on Creative outputs, second on Knowledge and technology outputs, third on Institutions, third on Market sophistication, fifth on Infrastructure, fifth on Business sophistication. On Human capital and research, Switzerland ranks sixth.

That is the pillar that signals the next ten years. Education, doctorates, researchers per capita, public and private research spending. Precisely where the future is built, we are weakest.

A comparison of research intensity reveals the pattern. Switzerland spent 3.31 per cent of GDP on R&D in 2021, historically between 3.1 and 3.4 per cent. Sweden reached 3.57 per cent in 2024. Belgium 3.36 per cent. Austria 3.26 per cent. Sweden has overtaken us on research intensity. Belgium and Austria are within reach.

The European Innovation Scoreboard 2025 likewise places Switzerland first in Europe for the eighth consecutive year, with 169.3 points. But the score is slightly below the previous year. At regional level, Stockholm, Hovedstaden, and London now lead. Zurich is fourth, Ticino in the top ten.

## Patents say less than they appear to

Patents per million inhabitants, 2023 data: Korea 3,696, Japan 1,839, Switzerland 1,212, China 1,079, United States 824. We are clearly ahead of the United States. But Korea has three times as many patents per capita, and the United States produces around 518,000 patents in absolute terms against 11,000 for Switzerland. Scale matters, because research ecosystems live on critical mass.

Patents per capita are a vanity metric. They measure activity, not effect, and they capture neither open-source contributions nor industrial manufacturing capability nor standards-setting power. Israel, for example, appears in the statistics with around 170 patents per million, because its inventors mostly file abroad. Domestic innovative capacity is much higher.

---

## The window is closing

By the time lagging indicators degrade, it is too late to act. Switzerland has the lead today to build the substrate for the next twenty years. In ten to fifteen years, that window is shut.

Concretely: decisions on sovereign compute infrastructure, on talent policy, on procurement rules, on energy and manufacturing reserves, must be taken in this legislative term and the next. Later corrections are more expensive and slower, because clusters, supply chains, and talent flows have inertia. A university that loses its international pull does not regain it within one legislative term. A datacentre that does not exist cannot be built by decree.

---

## What this means for our programme

The Future has argued from the start for digital sovereignty grounded in open standards, transparency, and security. The substrate gap is the reason for that position, not a political slogan. Specifically, we call for:

- **Anti-lock-in as a procurement principle.** Federal, cantonal, and municipal procurement leaves vendor change always possible. Open data formats, documented interfaces, interchangeable components.
- **Sovereign compute capacity as a national objective.** An inventory of Swiss datacentre capacity, its energy connection, and its procurement paths, with measurable expansion targets.
- **Simpler talent inflow.** Researchers, engineers, and founders from third countries need shorter paths, not longer ones.
- **Maintain and increase research intensity.** 3.5 per cent of GDP as the next target, with a clear public component.
- **Create energy headroom.** Innovation and resilience need reserves, not scarcity.

These are not new demands. They are the consequence of a simple observation: the ranking we are proud of describes our past. The substrate we will be measured on is being built today.
