On 29 November 2026 Switzerland votes on four federal proposals: the federal decree on additional financing of the AHV old-age pension through VAT, the popular initiative "For a restriction of fireworks", the popular initiative "Yes to fair federal taxes for married couples too – end the discrimination of marriage!", and the amendment to the War Material Act. Four very different topics — and yet one shared question: at what level, and with what instrument, does Switzerland take its decisions? We do not issue a voting recommendation here. We show where the four proposals meet our program, and where, from our perspective, the honest tensions lie. This framing rests on Parliament's decisions; the Federal Council's official explanations appear in October.

---

## Proposal 1: additional AHV financing through VAT

In March 2024 voters accepted the 13th AHV pension. It is paid for the first time in December 2026 and costs around CHF 4.2 billion in the first year, close to 5.4 billion a year by 2040. On 19 June 2026 Parliament decided to fund it by raising VAT by 0.4 percentage points from 2028: the standard rate from 8.1 to 8.5 percent, the special rate for accommodation from 3.8 to 4 percent; the reduced rate for food and medicines stays at 2.6 percent. That yields around CHF 1.5 billion a year. Higher wage contributions, which were also on the table, were dropped by Parliament. Because VAT is anchored in the constitution, the vote is mandatory and needs a double majority of people and cantons. The National Council approved 108 to 85, the Council of States 28 to 13. The Federal Council and Parliament recommend acceptance.

Our program reads this proposal along two pillars.

**Basic income.** For us, a universal basic security is a foundation, not a gift — and it has to be financed by taxing growing value creation: income, consumption, wealth. Not through wages alone, when an ever larger share of value comes from automation. VAT as the lever for a pension everyone receives sits within that logic; the same lever is built into our basic-income calculator. Mechanically, the proposal is close to what we describe for a basic income.

**Open government.** The tension lies in the honesty of the arithmetic. 1.5 billion covers about a third of the cost; the rest stays open and weighs on the AHV fund, whose spending exceeds its revenue from 2026 without additional financing. Transparent budgets are not a detail for us but a standard: a benefit promise that is two-thirds unfunded is a stopgap. Whoever votes yes funds a part. Whoever votes no funds nothing for now — the pension is paid regardless.

So the question behind the proposal is not "VAT or not". It is: when does Switzerland put the full bill for the 13th pension on the table — and on what base is it paid in an automated economy?

## Proposal 2: fireworks initiative

The initiative by four animal-welfare organisations wants a nationwide ban on the sale and use of loud fireworks by private individuals. Quiet fireworks such as fountains or sparklers would remain allowed, and cantons could still authorise professional displays at events of supra-regional importance. More than a hundred municipalities already have restrictions. The Federal Council and Parliament recommend rejection, with no counter-proposal: cantons and municipalities can already limit fireworks in time and place. An indirect counter-proposal that would have banned only noise-only firecrackers in the Explosives Act failed in the National Council's final vote on 19 June 2026, 114 to 79. So the initiative goes to the people unchanged.

Our program reads this proposal along one pillar.

**Cities & housing.** We want cities to grow inward: denser, closer, with short distances. But density also means more people, pets and wildlife live within earshot of the same fireworks. In a densified Switzerland, consideration is not a matter of taste but part of quality of life. So the initiative names a real problem. The tension lies in the instrument and the level: a constitutional article for a question every municipality can settle for itself today — and more than a hundred already do. On the other hand, Parliament itself discarded every milder national rule. Whoever votes no relies on local rules. Whoever votes yes lifts the rule from the municipalities into the constitution.

So the question behind the proposal is not "fireworks yes or no". It is: at what level does Switzerland organise consideration — and what happens when the local level does not?

## Proposal 3: married-couples initiative

The Centre party's popular initiative wants to abolish the marriage penalty in direct federal tax through the constitution. Married couples would continue to be assessed jointly but could no longer be disadvantaged compared with unmarried couples. On 8 March 2026 voters accepted individual taxation with 54 percent, which solves the same problem with the opposite model: each person is taxed separately, regardless of civil status. The initiative would again prescribe a different system for federal tax. The Federal Council expects revenue losses of CHF 700 million to 1.4 billion a year. The Federal Council and Parliament recommend rejection — the National Council 99 to 92, the Council of States 24 to 21.

Our program reads this proposal along one pillar.

**Open government.** A state that enables rather than blocks has simple, transparent procedures. We share the initiative's goal — no disadvantage based on civil status. But that is exactly the goal Switzerland already decided in March. The tension is that the initiative does not decide the whether but the how: joint assessment at the federal level while the cantons switch to individual taxation — two logics for the same people. That costs revenue and simplicity. Whoever votes yes chooses the joint-assessment model and accepts the duplication. Whoever votes no lets the individual taxation decided in March take effect.

So the question behind the proposal is not "abolish the marriage penalty or not" — that is settled. It is: which model should apply, and how many parallel systems can a simple state bear?

## Proposal 4: amendment to the War Material Act

Parliament wants to allow the Federal Council to deviate from the statutory licensing criteria for war material exports in extraordinary circumstances, to safeguard Switzerland's foreign or security policy interests. In 2021 Parliament had written those criteria firmly into law — no exports to countries in armed conflict, for example — and declined such an exception clause; the "correction initiative" was withdrawn as a result. Now the clause returns, justified by the situation since 2022 and the concerns of the Swiss security industry. The National Council approved 122 to 68, the Council of States 31 to 11. A referendum by the SP, the Greens, the EVP, the GSoA and aid organisations brings the proposal to the people. The Federal Council and Parliament recommend acceptance.

Our program reads this proposal along two pillars.

**Neutrality & peace.** Our guardrails are clear: strictly defensive, neutral and credible, transparent and bound by the rule of law — clear mandates, clear responsibilities, democratic control. The tension sits exactly there: the proposal moves a decision that the law takes today into the Federal Council's discretion. In a crisis that can be faster. But it makes Swiss practice harder to predict — and predictability is the capital of a neutral country that wants to mediate.

**Energy & infrastructure resilience.** The other side: protective capability needs an industrial base. Whoever wants to reduce critical dependences in components, spare parts and know-how needs firms that produce in the country — and they only do so if they can also export and cooperate with European partners. Since 2022, firms report that partners avoid Swiss components. That concern is real. Whether the answer is a discretionary clause for the executive or clearer rules set by Parliament is the actual question.

So the question behind the proposal is not "arms exports yes or no". It is: how much flexibility does Switzerland need — and who answers for it: the law or the Federal Council?

## How we frame this

Four proposals, four topics — and one shared pattern: each moves a decision to a different level. The fireworks initiative lifts a municipal rule into the constitution. The married-couples initiative places a second model next to a law just adopted. The War Material Act shifts a statutory rule into the Federal Council's discretion. And the AHV proposal funds part of a promise whose remainder stays open.

Our yardstick is the same in all four cases: a rule must be clear, financed, democratically controllable — and located where it works best. That is not a question of left or right. It is the question of whether the state stays simple, honest and reliable.

Our contribution is not to tell people where to put the cross. Our contribution is to describe the long-term substance by which Switzerland will be measured over the next twenty years. We have summarised it in our [program](@/programm.en.md) as twelve pillars. The four proposals on 29 November touch five of them. Whoever votes can take this reading as one voice among many.
