Under the electricity act that voters approved with 68.7% on 9 June 2024, households, businesses and communes have been able to form local electricity communities (LEG, *lokale Elektrizitätsgemeinschaften*) since 1 January 2026: power from the roof next door, over the public grid, with a discount on the grid tariff. By the end of June 2026 there were an estimated 1,800 LEGs, but very unevenly spread; some grid areas had none at all. Whether an LEG gets off the ground today depends heavily on the local grid operator, and most of them are owned by communes and cantons.

---

## Methodology

This note is based on the Electricity Supply Act (StromVG Art. 17d and 17e) and the Electricity Supply Ordinance (StromVV Art. 19e–19h, version of 1 July 2026), on Swissolar's survey of the 30 largest grid operators (as of end of June 2026) and on documented examples. The figure of about 1,800 LEGs is a Swissolar extrapolation from 1,323 recorded communities covering about 71% of the population. Savings examples come partly from initiators or utilities and are not independent measurements.

---

### What an LEG is

- **Who:** final consumers, renewable producers and storage operators in the same grid area, at the same grid level (up to 36 kV), close to each other, at most within one commune. There is no minimum or maximum number of members.
- **Condition:** generation capacity must be at least 5% of the connection power of the participating consumers. Everyone needs a smart meter from the grid operator, a written agreement and one representative towards the utility.
- **Discount:** 40% on the grid-use tariff for electricity that flows within the LEG, or 20% if a transformation between voltage levels is needed. The law allows up to 60%. System services, the electricity reserve, the grid surcharge and levies are not discounted.
- **Difference from a ZEV:** a self-consumption group (ZEV, since 2018; virtual ZEV since 2025) shares electricity over private lines and pays no grid fee for it, but is limited to one site. An LEG reaches across the neighbourhood up to the commune boundary.

### What it brings

In 2027 a median household pays about 26.5 centimes per kilowatt hour, of which 10.9 centimes are grid use (ElCom). The discount is therefore worth at most about 4.4 centimes per kilowatt hour from the LEG, or about 2.2 centimes at 20%. For consumers that means a few dozen to a few hundred francs a year, depending on the case. It is more attractive for producers, who get more within the LEG than the statutory minimum feed-in payment, and for communes with large roofs.

- **Rickenbach (ZH):** since 1 May 2026, about 40 participants including the commune hall and school, 127 kWp, operated by EKZ. Just over six months passed from the idea in autumn 2025 to the start; sign-up is online.
- **Lupsingen (BL):** 68 members, one of the largest LEGs. According to the initiator about 20% cheaper than the standard tariff; buyers together saved about CHF 4,400 since April.
- **Laupersdorf (SO):** a utility simulation for 15 municipal buildings and a 220 kWp school roof shows CHF 5,200 lower electricity costs a year (−19.7%).

### Where it gets stuck

- **Fees:** several grid operators charge setup fees, in some cases over CHF 500. The Confederation wants to stop this with an ordinance revision (new Art. 12a StromVV: costs treated as grid costs, not billed individually); the consultation ran until July 2026.
- **Processes and IT:** utilities are on new ground; billing and data exchange are laborious, and a common industry standard for data delivery is not finished yet. EKZ itself calls the effort "considerable".
- **Smart meters:** at the end of 2024 just over half of metering points had a smart meter; by the end of 2027 it must be 80%. On request the utility must install one within three months.
- **Commune boundary:** an LEG ends at the commune boundary and on one grid level; across levels the discount drops to 20%.

### What an LEG is not

An LEG is a billing model on the public grid, not an emergency power supply. If the grid fails, grid-connected solar systems switch off; island operation needs storage and switch-over equipment in the building. And because solar power comes mainly in summer, an LEG does not solve the winter question. Its contribution to resilience: electricity and money stay local, communes become less exposed to price swings, and consumption that follows local production can relieve the grid.

---

## What this means for policy

Most grid operators are fully or partly publicly owned. Communes and cantons can act at once through the owner strategy of their utility, without waiting for Bern:

- **No fees:** no setup, joining or exit fees, even before the federal ordinance requires it.
- **Publish the grid map:** publish transformer areas and grid levels as a map, so anyone can see straight away who can form an LEG with whom.
- **Online in one step:** sign-up with standard terms and the model contract from SwissEnergy and the industry; billing by the utility at cost; data free of charge in the standard format.
- **Smart meters first where LEGs are forming**, within weeks rather than months.
- **The commune as anchor:** school and municipal roofs as producers, municipal buildings as large consumers, open to residents, following the Rickenbach model.
- **Measure:** every utility reports each year the number of LEGs, members and shared kilowatt hours: [Energy & Infrastructure Resilience](@/energy-resilience.en.md).
- **At federal level:** use the legal scope of a 60% discount and allow LEGs across grid levels and commune boundaries.
